# Page body

## Overview
The exchange of notes of 9 and 10 June 1939 between the United States Minister in Ottawa, Daniel C. Roper, and the Canadian Under-Secretary of State for External Affairs, O. D. Skelton, records agreed interpretations of the 1817 [Rush-Bagot Agreement](https://app.notion.com/p/28d2b9a41a30809f8e39cc46922d634e) on naval forces on the Great Lakes. Rather than amend an agreement whose technical scheme both governments regarded as out of date, they agreed that certain practical measures were consistent with its underlying objective: free movement of naval vessels within the Lakes, reserve training and target practice in each party's own territorial waters, two 4-inch guns on each of three training vessels in the summer season, and the construction in Great Lakes shipyards of naval vessels intended only for tidewater service, subject to advance information, removal on completion and no armament until the seaboard. Canada's reply states its assumption that the understandings apply equally to Canadian vessels and shipyards; the United States did not separately confirm this in the exchange. The exchange entered into force on 10 June 1939 and is listed as in force in the US [Treaties in Force](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Treaties-in-Force-2025-FINAL.pdf) as of 1 January 2025. Further interpretive exchanges followed in 1940, 1942 and 1946.

## Provisions and obligations
The instrument has no numbered articles. Citations refer to paragraphs counted consecutively in the [Roper note of 9 June 1939 and the Skelton note of 10 June 1939](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=358) (UNTS vol. 149, No. 476; also [Canada Treaty Information E101663](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101663&lang=eng)). Headings (1) to (4) are those of the notes. The parties described these measures as consistent with the underlying objective of the 1817 agreement, though in part not strictly consistent with its technical scheme or definitions (Skelton note, para. 3); they are interpretations, not amendments.

### Disposition and functions of vessels
- The naval vessels of each party may move freely in the Great Lakes and be maintained at any of its ports there, notwithstanding the 1817 allocation of vessels by lake (Roper note, para. 11; Skelton note, para. 6).
- Employing the vessels of both parties in reserve training or any other normal activity, including target practice, is within the letter and spirit of the agreement when done within each party's own territorial waters (Roper note, para. 12; Skelton note, para. 7).

### Armament
- Two 4-inch guns may be placed on each of three US naval vessels on the Lakes, with all other armament removed (Roper note, para. 15; Skelton note, para. 8). The 1817 text allows one 18-pound cannon per vessel; Roper noted that a 4-inch gun fires a shell of about 30 pounds (para. 14).
- Conditions: target practice is confined to the party's territorial waters, and the 4-inch guns are dismantled except in the summer season while naval reserves are trained (Roper note, para. 15; Skelton note, para. 8).
- Canada stated its assumption that it would be told the names of the vessels so armed and given particulars of any later change in the armament of the five US vessels (Skelton note, para. 8).

### Construction of naval vessels on the Lakes
- Either country may permit naval vessels intended only for tidewater service to be built in Great Lakes shipyards (Roper note, paras. 16 to 19; Skelton note, para. 9), reading the 1817 bar on building vessels of war on the Lakes in the light of the then absence of a navigable connection to the ocean (Roper note, paras. 17 and 18).
- Conditions: full information to the other government before construction begins; immediate removal from the Lakes on completion; no armament installed until the vessel reaches the seaboard (Roper note, para. 19; Skelton note, para. 9). Removal through the other party's waters requires the usual request through diplomatic channels (Skelton note, para. 9).
- The no-armament condition was relaxed by the [further interpretation of 30 October and 2 November 1940](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101664&lang=eng), a separate instrument.

### Number of vessels
- Canada agreed to the substitution of the Sacramento for the Hawk, assuming it would receive information on both vessels and a formal request for passage (Roper note, para. 9; Skelton note, para. 5).
- The exchange sets no new ceiling on the number or size of vessels. The United States stated that it did not propose to exceed its five vessels, excluding vessels retained immobile as floating barracks, and that it would continue to station only unclassified vessels with a draft of not more than fourteen feet (Roper note, paras. 8 and 10); these are statements of US policy, not reciprocal undertakings. Canada's reply noted only that no increase in the number of US vessels was proposed (Skelton note, para. 5).

### Bound parties
- Although several measures are stated for US vessels, Canada's reply states its assumption that the observations and understandings apply equally to Canadian vessels on the Lakes and to vessels built in Canadian shipyards (Skelton note, para. 10); the United States did not separately confirm this in the exchange. The measures are coded as applying to both parties on that basis, except the Sacramento substitution, which concerns the United States alone, and the information on vessels fitted with 4-inch guns, which Canada assumed the United States would supply (Skelton note, para. 8).

## Participation and legal operation
- **Form and parties.** A bilateral exchange of notes at Ottawa between the United States Minister to Canada, Daniel C. Roper (9 June 1939), and the Canadian Under-Secretary of State for External Affairs, O. D. Skelton (10 June 1939) ([Canada Treaty Information, details](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/details.aspx?id=101663&lang=eng)). Roper's note asked for Canada's observations for Secretary Hull's "informal and confidential information" (Roper note, para. 20), and Skelton called it an "informal letter" (Skelton note, para. 1). Both governments nonetheless published it as a treaty: Canada in the Canada Treaty Series (1940 No. 12) and the United States in TIAS 1836 and 61 Stat. 4069, as listed in [Treaties in Force 2025](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Treaties-in-Force-2025-FINAL.pdf) (Canada, Defense, p. 62). The [Foreign Relations of the United States](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1939v02/ch13subch8) series calls it an understanding effected by exchange of notes.
- **Relationship to the 1817 agreement.** It interprets the [Rush-Bagot Agreement](https://app.notion.com/p/28d2b9a41a30809f8e39cc46922d634e) and does not amend it.
- **Entry into force.** No requirement is stated. The exchange came into force on 10 June 1939 by the exchange of the notes ([UNTS vol. 149, p. 334, note 1](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=358)).
- **Duration and withdrawal.** The notes state no term and contain no withdrawal clause. They interpret the 1817 agreement, which either party may terminate on six months' notice; the notes do not say how such a termination would affect these interpretations. The 4-inch guns may be mounted only in the summer training season (Skelton note, para. 8).
- **Amendment and later practice.** There is no amendment clause. Canada stated that new practical measures should be settled, as in the past, by consultation and correspondence (Skelton note, para. 3), and the 1940 notes record that this procedure had been agreed ([Skelton note of 30 October 1940, para. 2](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101664&lang=eng)).
- **Review and depositary.** No review mechanism and no depositary. The United States filed and recorded the exchange with the UN Secretariat on 6 November 1952 ([UNTS vol. 149, No. 476](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=356)).
- **Status.** Listed as in force in Treaties in Force as of 1 January 2025.

## History and compliance

### Origins
On 31 January 1939 the Acting Secretary of the Navy, Admiral Leahy, asked Secretary of State Hull in a confidential letter whether two 4-inch guns could be mounted on each US naval vessel on the Great Lakes for reserve target practice, and if not, whether the Rush-Bagot Agreement could be modified (Roper note of 9 June 1939, para. 1, [UNTS vol. 149, p. 334](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=358); the letter itself was not printed by the Department of State, note 4). Hull concluded that modification was undesirable: the agreement's provisions had long been out of date from a naval standpoint, but it had acquired symbolic importance, and drafts of changes exchanged after the First World War had never been agreed (Roper note, para. 2).

### The US note and the Canadian reply
Roper's note acknowledged that the agreement had "from time to time" been subjected to "what may have been considered technical violations by both parties", and that the five US vessels then on the Lakes, all over 100 tons, "may not be considered entirely in keeping with a literal interpretation", while noting that they were there with Canada's knowledge (Roper note, paras. 3 to 5 and 10). These are qualified US statements, not an acknowledged breach. Roper then proposed interpretations under four headings and on shipbuilding ([UNTS, pp. 338-342](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=362)).
Skelton replied on 10 June 1939 after consulting the Acting Prime Minister and Secretary of State for External Affairs and the Department of National Defence. Canada agreed that formal revision was impracticable, accepted each measure as consistent with the agreement's underlying objective, and stated its assumption that the understandings would apply equally to Canadian vessels and shipyards (Skelton note, paras. 1 to 10, [UNTS, pp. 344-348](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=368); [Canada Treaty Information E101663](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101663&lang=eng)).

### Later operation
On 30 October and 2 November 1940 the governments agreed a further interpretation, expressly referring to the 1939 notes, that allowed armament to be installed on vessels built on the Lakes if it was incapable of immediate use while they remained there ([Canada Treaty Information E101664](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101664&lang=eng)). The Canadian note of 18 November 1946 recalled that notes had been exchanged in 1939, 1940 and 1942 and that both governments recognised the agreement's detailed provisions as inapplicable to present-day conditions ([Canada Treaty Information E101665](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101665&lang=eng); [UNTS vol. 149, p. 3](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=27)). The United States filed and recorded the 1939, 1940 and 1942 exchanges with the UN Secretariat on 6 November 1952 ([UNTS vol. 149, No. 476](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=356)).
Whether the United States fitted 4-inch guns on three vessels, replaced the Hawk with the Sacramento, or notified Canada of the vessels concerned was not established in the sources reviewed. No compliance allegation or formal finding concerning this exchange was identified in the sources reviewed.

### Coding notes
- **Title.** Taken from Canada's Treaty Information record (E101663), which describes the whole exchange. US Treaties in Force 2025 lists it as "Agreement relating to the construction of naval vessels on the Great Lakes (interpretation of the Rush-Bagot Agreement)", and UNTS vol. 149 (No. 476) as an exchange of notes relating to the application and interpretation of the 1817 agreement with respect to the construction of naval vessels; both titles name only one of its subjects.
- **Legal character.** Coded legally binding. The notes are styled as informal letters (Roper note, para. 20; Skelton note, para. 1), but Canada published the exchange in its Treaty Series, the United States published it in TIAS and the Statutes at Large and filed it with the UN Secretariat as an agreement, and it is listed in Treaties in Force.
- **Relationship.** Coded as interpreting the Rush-Bagot Agreement because both governments presented the measures as consistent with the 1817 agreement's objective rather than as a revision (Roper note, para. 2; Skelton note, paras. 2 and 3), and the Canadian title calls it an exchange relating to the application and interpretation of that agreement.
- **Dates.** The single adoption and signature date carries 10 June 1939, when the exchange was completed, following the convention for the parent exchange of notes; the first note is dated 9 June 1939.
- **Consultation.** Coded "Diplomatic channels only": the Skelton note (para. 3) states that new practical measures should be settled by consultation and correspondence, and the 1940 Skelton note (para. 2) records that this procedure was agreed.
- **Reporting.** Coded "Yes" under the codebook definition (data submitted to another party): each government is to give the other full information on naval vessels to be built at Great Lakes ports before construction begins (Roper note, para. 19; Skelton note, para. 9). Information provisions are coded as notifications, which also covers Canada's assumption that it would be told of vessels fitted with 4-inch guns (Skelton note, para. 8).
- **Other Yes/No fields.** Dispute resolution, enforcement, national authority, technical cooperation and third-party roles are "Not specified in text", from the instrument's own text. The consultation procedure concerns new practical measures, not disputes.
- **Duration and withdrawal.** Not specified and none stated in these notes; the parent agreement's six-month termination clause is not repeated.
- **Subject matter.** Risk reduction and confidence-building measures are added for the advance-information requirement on shipbuilding; the main subject remains arms limitation. Operating environment follows the parent record (Sea, for naval forces on inland lakes).
- **Provision rows.** Measures are coded as applying to both parties on the basis of Canada's stated assumption of equal application (Skelton note, para. 10), which the United States did not separately confirm. The Sacramento substitution and the information on 4-inch gun vessels are coded for the United States alone. US statements on not increasing its five vessels and on stationing only unclassified vessels of not more than fourteen feet draft (Roper note, paras. 8 and 10) are US policy and are not coded as provisions. The armament row's value of 2 is guns per vessel; the limit of three vessels is stated in its unit.
- **Events.** The 9 June 1939 note is coded "Other" and the completion of the exchange on 10 June 1939 as adoption. Roper's reference to possible technical violations is described in prose, not coded as an acknowledged breach, because it is expressly qualified.
- **Size.** 3,318 words; paragraphs are counted consecutively within each note (Roper 20, Skelton 10) because the notes are not numbered.
