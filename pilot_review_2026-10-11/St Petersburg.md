# Page body

## Overview
The St Petersburg Declaration of 29 November (11 December) 1868 is the first formal agreement prohibiting the use of a particular weapon in war ([ICRC](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868)). Seventeen governments whose representatives had met in an International Military Commission convened by Russia signed it, and Baden and Brazil acceded in 1869. The parties renounce, in wars among themselves, the use by their land or naval forces of any projectile weighing less than 400 grammes that is explosive or charged with fulminating or inflammable substances, a rule aimed at explosive and incendiary rifle bullets. The engagement binds only parties in wars between parties and lapses if a non-party joins a belligerent. The ICRC lists it as in force, although its customary IHL study reports that later practice narrowed the rule to the anti-personnel use of bullets that explode within the human body. Its preamble, which states that the only legitimate object of war is to weaken the military forces of the enemy, is widely cited as an early statement of the prohibition of unnecessary suffering.

## Provisions and obligations
The Declaration has no numbered articles. Citations refer to the introductory paragraph, the five preambular considering clauses and the five operative paragraphs of the [authentic French text](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/fr/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868/declaration), checked against the [1873 printing in Martens, Nouveau recueil général, vol. 18, no. 116](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ). The ICRC [English translation](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868/declaration) follows the same order.

### Preamble
- The Commission is said to have fixed by common agreement "the technical limits at which the necessities of war ought to yield to the requirements of humanity" (preamble, para. 1).
- The considering clauses state that the only legitimate object of war is to weaken the military forces of the enemy, that disabling the greatest possible number of men suffices, and that arms which uselessly aggravate the sufferings of disabled men or render their death inevitable are contrary to the laws of humanity (considering clauses 1 to 5).

### The prohibition
- The contracting parties renounce, in case of war among themselves, the employment by their military or naval troops of any projectile weighing less than 400 grammes that is explosive or charged with fulminating or inflammable substances (operative para. 1).
- The rule restricts use in war only. The Commission chose the word "employment" rather than "armament" so as not to forbid peacetime trials ([Protocol No. 2](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)). The text excludes projectiles weighing 400 grammes or more; coverage turns on weight and contents, not on the type of weapon firing them. Historically the threshold was set to separate rifle bullets from artillery shells (Protocol No. 1).

### Bound parties and the general participation clause
- The engagement binds contracting and acceding parties identically, but only in a war between two or more of them, and does not apply towards non-parties (operative para. 3).
- It ceases to be compulsory once a non-contracting or non-acceding party joins one of the belligerents (operative para. 4). The British commissioner asked for this wording at the second session ([Protocol No. 2](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)).

### Accession and future developments
- The parties will invite states that did not send delegates to the Commission to accede (operative para. 2).
- The parties reserve the right to reach a further understanding whenever a precise proposition is made about future improvements in armament, in order to maintain the principles they have established (operative para. 5). The Russian Foreign Ministry delegate told the Commission that this left any further understanding optional ([Protocol No. 2](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)).
- The text contains no provisions on information, verification, consultation, dispute settlement or enforcement.

### Later interpretation
The text has not been amended. The ICRC customary IHL study ([Rule 78](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule78)) reports that exploding anti-aircraft bullets were introduced in the First World War and lighter grenades and exploding anti-materiel bullets later, without objection, so that state practice now treats only the anti-personnel use of bullets that explode within the human body as prohibited. Article 18 of the [1923 Hague Rules of Air Warfare](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/hague-rules-1923), never adopted as a treaty, stated that explosive projectiles by or against aircraft are not forbidden.

## Participation and legal operation
- **Signature.** Representatives of 17 governments signed at St Petersburg on 29 November (11 December) 1868 ([Martens, NRG, vol. 18, no. 116](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)). The text provides no signature period and no ratification clause.
- **Entry into force.** No entry-into-force clause. The [ICRC](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868) gives 11 December 1868 and records each signatory's ratification or accession on that date. At the first session the Bavarian and Württemberg representatives spoke of acting subject to ratification; whether any ratification followed was not established.
- **Accession.** Open to states that did not send delegates to the Commission (operative para. 2). The ICRC lists Baden (11 January 1869) and Brazil (23 October 1869) and no later accession. The ICRC customary IHL study states that Estonia adhered in 1991, but the ICRC treaty database does not list Estonia and no instrument was found, so Estonia is not counted.
- **Participants.** 19 listed by the ICRC (list checked 11 October 2026): 17 original signatories and two accessions in 1869. This is a historical count, not a count of current states parties; several rows leave present-day participation unresolved. The list includes seven historical entities that the ICRC lists without successors (Austria-Hungary, Baden, Bavaria, Persia, Prussia and the North German Confederation, Sweden and Norway, and Württemberg). The ICRC lists the Ottoman and Russian participations under Türkiye and the Russian Federation; this record reports those listings and does not code successions.
- **Application.** Only in a war between parties; the engagement lapses if a non-party joins a belligerent (operative paras. 3 and 4).
- **Duration and withdrawal.** No term and no withdrawal or denunciation clause.
- **Amendment and review.** No amendment procedure and no review conferences; operative para. 5 reserves an optional further understanding on new weapons.
- **Depositary.** None designated; the custody of the signed original was not established.

## History and compliance

### Origins
Russia introduced an explosive rifle bullet in 1863, intended for blowing up ammunition wagons, and in 1867 a version that exploded on contact with soft targets ([ICRC introduction](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868)). Keefer reports that Prussia, Austria, Switzerland and Bavaria also developed such bullets ([Keefer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0968344513505810)). By circular of 9 (21) May 1868 Russia proposed an international ban; a draft protocol followed on 17 (29) June and, after Prussia asked for a broader inquiry, a third circular on 5 (17) July convened a military commission ([Russian memorandum, NRG, vol. 18, no. 115](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)).

### The International Military Commission
Chaired by War Minister Dmitry Milutin, the Commission met on 28 October (9 November), 1 (13) November and 4 (16) November 1868 ([Protocols Nos. 1 to 3](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_K4oBAAAAYAAJ)). It set aside the Prussian idea of a general rule, adopted the 400-gramme limit separating banned projectiles from artillery shells, extended the ban to incendiary projectiles and, at British request, added the general participation clause. Keefer argues that Britain insisted on reciprocity to keep such weapons available against non-European adversaries and reports that London authorized signature only after confirming the Declaration would not apply to Central Asia ([Keefer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0968344513505810)). Spain and the United States did not take part. The Declaration was signed on 29 November (11 December) 1868, and Baden and Brazil acceded in 1869 ([ICRC states parties](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/st-petersburg-decl-1868/state-parties)).

### Compliance
Keefer reports that claims of violations were made during the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871 and the Anglo-Transvaal war of 1880 to 1881; he gives no details, and no formal finding was identified in the sources reviewed ([Keefer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0968344513505810)).

### Later influence
The ICRC study states the prohibition was repeated in the 1874 Brussels Declaration ([Rule 78](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule78)). The three 1899 Hague Declarations, including the [Declaration on expanding bullets](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/hague-decl-iv-3-1899), cite in their preambles the sentiments of the St Petersburg Declaration; Keefer reports that delegates first considered a protocol to the 1868 Declaration before choosing separate instruments. Article 18 of the [1923 Hague Rules of Air Warfare](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/hague-rules-1923) allowed explosive projectiles against aircraft. In 1999 ICRC-convened experts agreed that targeting combatants with 12.7 mm bullets foreseeably exploding in the body would be contrary to the Declaration's object and purpose, and the 2005 customary IHL study (Rule 78) treats the anti-personnel use of such bullets as prohibited ([Rule 78](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule78)). The ICRC marked the 140th and 150th anniversaries at conferences in St Petersburg in [2008](http://web.archive.org/web/20090701123820/http://www.icrc.org:80/web/eng/siteeng0.nsf/html/st-petersburg-declaration-281108) and [2018](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-president-address-150th-anniversary-st-petersburg-declaration).

### Coding notes
- **Form and legal character.** Coded as a declaration, the parties' own title, but legally binding: the contracting parties "engage mutually", the engagement is "compulsory" for contracting and acceding parties (operative paras. 1 and 3), and the ICRC describes the Declaration as having the force of law.
- **Dates.** Date Adopted records formal adoption of the text, here the signature of 11 December 1868 (Gregorian) that concluded the Declaration, given in the document as 29 November (11 December) 1868 because Russia used the Julian calendar, then twelve days behind. The International Military Commission had approved the wording earlier, at its third session on 4 (16) November 1868; that preparatory approval is recorded as an event, not as adoption. The key facts say "Signed" because the text records a single signature, with no opening for signature. There was no signature period, and the text has no entry-into-force or ratification clause; entry into force on 11 December 1868 follows the ICRC.
- **Parties.** No current-party count is coded. The ICRC lists 19 participants, counted on 11 October 2026: 17 original signatories plus the accessions of Baden (11 January 1869) and Brazil (23 October 1869); the ICRC customary IHL study likewise describes nineteen states adhering in 1868-69. The historical count is not used as a count of current states parties. The seven historical entities are counted as the ICRC lists them, with no successor counted. The Ottoman and Russian participations are counted under Türkiye and the Russian Federation as the ICRC lists them. Estonia, reported in the customary IHL study, is not counted. The 17 signatures match those printed in Martens; the Commission had 16 delegations at its first session, and Persia joined at the second.
- **Participation structure.** Multilateral (open): operative para. 2 invites all states that did not send delegates to accede.
- **Operating environment.** Land and Sea, from "military or naval troops" ("troupes de terre ou de mer"), added at Belgium's proposal at the second session. Air warfare did not exist in 1868; later treatment of aircraft is described in prose.
- **Weapon families.** The rule is defined by projectile weight and effect, not by launcher. Small arms and light weapons reflects its target, explosive bullets for rifles and mitrailleuses (the Commission's president said artillery was excluded, Protocol No. 1); incendiary weapons reflects the words "fulminating or inflammable substances", added to cover incendiary bullets (Protocol No. 2).
- **Yes/No fields.** Third-party roles are coded "Yes" because operative para. 2 provides for inviting states that were not at the Commission to accede (the codebook's non-parties that may accept the instrument). Reporting, enforcement, dispute resolution, national authority and technical cooperation are "Not specified in text". The general participation clause limits when the rule applies but provides no consequence for non-compliance. All are coded from the instrument's own text.
- **Consultation, duration, withdrawal and amendment.** No consultation mechanism: operative para. 5 reserves an optional future understanding on new armaments, which the Russian Foreign Ministry delegate described as facultative (Protocol No. 2). Duration is indefinite; there is no withdrawal or denunciation clause, and the lapse when a non-party joins a war (operative para. 4) is a condition of application, not withdrawal. No amendment procedure.
- **Status.** In force, as recorded by the ICRC. The customary IHL study reports that later practice modified the prohibition (exploding anti-aircraft and anti-materiel bullets), and Kalshoven considers that customary law set aside the treaty rule for aircraft; these are described as later interpretation, not termination.
- **Source quality.** Reproduction: the texts used are the ICRC's reproduction (French and English translation, from Schindler and Toman) and the 1873 printing in Martens. No depositary or government treaty-series text was consulted.
- **Related instruments.** The Declaration was produced by the International Military Commission, which has no record of its own. No relation to the 1899 Hague Declaration (IV,3) is coded: it cites this Declaration's sentiments in its preamble but is a separate instrument, not a protocol, amendment or replacement, and the two were not negotiated together.
- **Provision rows.** The renunciation is one row because its explosive, fulminating and inflammable limbs bind identically; both weapon families are tagged. The general participation clause and the reserved future understanding are not separate obligations on a weapon activity and appear in exceptions and prose.

    - ICRC data were read from the site's JSON:API (the public pages are a JavaScript app): node f4117bde-d283-4925-a144-4e12e1674f11 (field_treaty_entry_in_force 1868-12-11, field_treaty_in_force yes, forum International Military Commission, authentic text French, source Schindler and Toman 1988 p. 102; last changed 2020-12-11) and its field_treaty_state_parties list.
