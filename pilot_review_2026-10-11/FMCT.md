# Page body

## Overview
A Fissile Material Cut-off Treaty would ban the production of fissile material (chiefly highly enriched uranium and plutonium) for nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices. No treaty has been concluded. A negotiating committee met briefly in 1998, but sustained negotiations have not followed. The Conference on Disarmament agreed a negotiating mandate in 1995, but the 65-member body works by consensus and has not been able to start or sustain negotiations, above all because states disagree on whether existing stocks should be covered. Since 2012 the work has moved to expert groups and discussion-only bodies. As of October 2026 the effort is stalled.

## The negotiating mandate
There is no FMCT text, so there are no obligations, parties or provision rows to record. The only agreed negotiating text is the mandate in CD/1299 (24 March 1995), reaffirmed by CD/1547 (1998) and CD/1864 (2009). It is a procedural mandate that tells a committee what to negotiate; it binds no state to any restriction.
- **Object:** a "ban on the production of fissile material for nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices" (mandate para. 1).
- **Character:** the treaty is to be "non-discriminatory, multilateral and internationally and effectively verifiable" (para. 2), wording taken from UN General Assembly resolution 48/75 L.
- **Reporting:** the committee was to report on progress before the end of the 1995 session (para. 3). It did not convene that year.
- **Built-in ambiguity:** the report records that some delegations read the mandate as covering only future production, others as also covering past production, and others as extending to the management of material. It states that the mandate "does not preclude any delegation from raising" these issues (CD/1299, p. 2).

## Open questions
- **Existing stocks.** The central dispute. A production-only ban would leave existing military stocks untouched. Pakistan has argued that this would lock in an advantage for India and insists on covering stocks; several non-aligned states, including Cuba in 2025, have argued that a treaty leaving stockpiles uncontrolled would be insufficient; most nuclear-weapon States have favoured a cut-off of future production. The expert reports (A/70/81 and A/73/159) set out options, from excluding stocks to covering some categories such as material released from weapons programmes, without choosing among them.
- **Definitions.** What counts as "fissile material" (only weapons-usable plutonium and highly enriched uranium, or other materials too) and what counts as "production" (enrichment and reprocessing only, or other processes).
- **Naval and other non-explosive military uses.** Whether material for naval propulsion would be exempt and, if so, how it would be accounted for.
- **Verification.** Whether verification would cover all relevant facilities or focus on enrichment and reprocessing plants, what role the IAEA would play, and how classified information would be protected. In 2006 the United States tabled a draft treaty without verification provisions; it later, under President Obama, returned to supporting a verifiable treaty.
- **Institutions, entry into force and duration.** The expert reports outline options for a treaty organization or reliance on the IAEA, for entry-into-force conditions (including whether specified states must ratify), and for duration and withdrawal. None of these has been negotiated.

## Who a treaty would affect
Non-nuclear-weapon States party to the NPT are already barred from acquiring nuclear explosives and accept safeguards, so a cut-off would add little for them. It would bind the nuclear-armed states. Four NPT nuclear-weapon States (the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom and France) have declared that they no longer produce fissile material for weapons; China is widely reported to have stopped but has made no official declaration. These are unilateral political statements, not obligations. On one analytical reading, a treaty would therefore mainly constrain the states outside the NPT that are reported to continue production (India, Pakistan, Israel and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea) and, if stocks were included, the existing holdings of all nuclear-armed states. How much it would change depends on its scope and verification; even where production has already stopped, it would turn unilateral restraint into a verifiable obligation.

## Negotiating history

### Origins
Proposals to halt the production of fissile material for weapons date to the 1950s. The current effort began with a speech by President Clinton to the General Assembly in September 1993 and with resolution 48/75 L, adopted by consensus on 16 December 1993, which recommended negotiation of a verifiable treaty. The Conference on Disarmament appointed Ambassador Gerald Shannon of Canada as Special Coordinator in January 1994 and in March 1995 accepted his report and mandate (CD/1299). The 1995 NPT Review and Extension Conference also called for the early conclusion of such a treaty.

### Stalemate in the Conference on Disarmament
- **1995 to 1998.** Disputes over linkage to other agenda items, especially nuclear disarmament and the prevention of an arms race in outer space, prevented the ad hoc committee from meeting until August 1998 (CD/1547). It met only briefly, at the end of the 1998 session, and was not re-established.
- **2009.** The Conference adopted a programme of work (CD/1864) with a negotiating working group on the Shannon mandate but could not agree on implementing arrangements. Pakistan is widely reported to have withheld consensus, and has since stated that it will not join negotiations on a treaty confined to future production.
- **2012 to 2018.** Work moved to expert bodies created by the General Assembly: a Group of Governmental Experts under resolution 67/53, which reported in May 2015 (A/70/81), and a High-Level Expert Preparatory Group under resolution 71/259, which adopted its report in June 2018 (A/73/159). Both reports set out options and did not negotiate. In 2015 France tabled a draft treaty text in the Conference.
- **Subsidiary bodies.** The Conference established discussion-only subsidiary bodies in 2018 (CD/2119), 2022 (CD/2229), 2024 (CD/2390) and 2025 (CD/2443). Fissile material was discussed in them, but no negotiating mandate followed. In 2026 the Conference could not agree to reinstate them.

### Recent developments
In September 2024 twelve states launched the Friends of a Fissile Material Cut-off Treaty. In 2025 the General Assembly's First Committee handled the item through a procedural draft decision (A/C.1/80/L.39) rather than a substantive resolution. The 2026 NPT Review Conference ended on 22 May 2026 without a final document, and, according to Italy's mission to the Conference, the 2026 session (scheduled to close on 11 September) ended without a programme of work.

### Coding notes
- **Record type and status.** Coded as a negotiation, "Stalled", with legal character "Not established". It is not "Ongoing" because sustained negotiations have not taken place: the 1998 ad hoc committee met only briefly (the 2000 NPT Review Conference record describes its exchange of views as a first step in substantive negotiations), the 2009 working group never met, and in 2026 even the discussion-only bodies were not reinstated.
- **Treaty fields.** Dates, parties, depositary, duration, withdrawal, amendment, review and size are left blank because no treaty text exists; "None" or "Not specified" would wrongly imply a text that is silent. Source quality is "Not applicable (no concluded treaty text)" for the same reason; the mandate and process documents are official UN documents.
- **Weapon families.** Coded as fissile material and nuclear weapons, since the mandate concerns material for nuclear explosive devices. Whether existing stocks would be covered is unresolved, so no restriction type is coded.
- **Pakistan's role in 2009.** Attributed to secondary reporting (Arms Control Today and the Arms Control Association), not to Conference plenary records.
- **Draft treaties.** The 2006 US and 2015 French drafts appear in the narrative only; their document symbols and exact dates were not confirmed.

Historical Background
The FMCT has been negotiated for two decades
Nations already within the NPT are already prohibited from the production and acquisition of fissile material for weapons, “the FMCT would provide new restrictions for the five recognized nuclear weapon states (NWS—United States, Russia, United Kingdom, France, and China), and for the four nations that are not NPT members (Israel, India, Pakistan, and North Korea).”
“Efforts to curb the spread of nuclear material and technology began only a short time after the world was introduced to the destructive potential of atomic weaponry. In 1946 the Acheson-Lilienthal Report, authored in part by Manhattan Project physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer, advocated for an Atomic Development Agency to regulate fissile material and ensure that state rivalries over the technology did not occur. Ultimately, neither Dean Acheson or David Lilienthal presented the U.S. plan to the United Nations Atomic Energy Commission (UNAEC). Instead, Bernard Baruch presented the Baruch Plan, which also would have established an Atomic Development Authority that answered to the UN Security-Council. The plan called for the United States to disassemble its nuclear arsenal, but only after an agreement had been reached assuring the United States that the Soviets would not be able to acquire a bomb. The plan failed to achieve consensus within the UNAEC.
Much later, UN resolution 78/57 L, which passed unanimously in 1993, called for a “non-discriminatory, multi-lateral and internationally and effectively verifiable treaty banning the production of fissile material for nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices.”
In March 1995, the CD took up a mandate presented by Canadian Ambassador Gerald Shannon. The Shannon Mandate established an ad hoc committee that was directed to negotiate an FMCT by the end of the 1995 session. A lack of consensus over verification provisions, as well as desires to hold parallel negotiations on outer space arms control issues, prevented negotiations from getting underway. China and Russia articulated a desire to hold parallel negotiations on Preventing an Arms Race in Outer Space (PAROS), a point which has further stalled efforts to begin FMCT negotiations.”
