# Page body

## Overview
The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons is a framework treaty for restricting or banning specific conventional weapons considered excessively injurious or indiscriminate. The Convention itself restricts no weapon: it sets the scope, entry-into-force, amendment, review and denunciation rules, and the substantive restrictions sit in annexed Protocols, which states accept individually (at least two on joining). Adopted in 1980 with Protocols I to III, it has since added Protocols on blinding lasers (1995) and explosive remnants of war (2003), amended Protocol II on mines (1996), and extended its scope to non-international armed conflicts (2001). It has 128 parties, including all five permanent members of the Security Council, India, Pakistan and Israel, and since 2014 it has hosted the international discussions on lethal autonomous weapons systems.

## Provisions and obligations

### A framework without weapon restrictions
- The Convention contains no substantive weapons restrictions of its own; those are set out in the annexed Protocols (I to V), which are separate records.
- Any Protocol a party accepts "shall for that Party form an integral part of this Convention" (Art. 4(5)).
- Its only conduct obligation is dissemination: parties must disseminate the Convention and their accepted Protocols in peace and war and include them in military instruction (Art. 6).

### Scope (Arts. 1, 2 and 7)
- As concluded, the Convention and its Protocols apply in international armed conflicts under Art. 2 common to the 1949 Geneva Conventions, including the wars of national liberation described in Art. 1(4) of Additional Protocol I (Art. 1). The 2001 amendment extending the scope to non-international armed conflicts is a separate record.
- Nothing in the Convention detracts from other obligations under international humanitarian law (Art. 2).
- Parties bound by a Protocol remain bound in their mutual relations even if another party to the conflict is not (Art. 7(1)), and are bound towards a non-party that accepts and applies the Convention or Protocol and notifies the Depositary (Art. 7(2)).
- An authority of the type described in Art. 96(3) of Additional Protocol I may accept and apply the Convention in a conflict against a party (Art. 7(4)).

### Additional protocols and later decisions
- Additional protocols on other categories of conventional weapons may be adopted by a conference open to all states (Art. 8(2)). Protocols IV and V were adopted this way.
- The Third Review Conference (2006) adopted a compliance mechanism: consultation and cooperation on compliance concerns, and national reports on dissemination, legislation, technical measures and cooperation. The Fourth Review Conference (2011) set 31 March as the annual reporting deadline. These are political decisions, not treaty text.
- The text contains no verification, inspection, enforcement or dispute-settlement provisions.

## Participation and legal operation
The Convention was open for signature in New York for twelve months from 10 April 1981, and 50 states signed; it has since been open to accession (Arts. 3-4). A state must, when it joins, notify its consent to be bound by at least two of the annexed Protocols, and may accept others later (Art. 4(3)-(4)). Egypt, Nigeria, Sudan and Viet Nam have signed but not ratified; states that have neither signed nor acceded include Iran, Syria, Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, Ethiopia and Kenya.
- **Entry into force.** Six months after the twentieth instrument of ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, and for later states six months after their own deposit (Art. 5(1)-(2)). Each Protocol enters into force separately, six months after twenty states have consented to it (Art. 5(3)-(4)). The Convention entered into force on 2 December 1983.
- **Duration and denunciation.** No term is fixed. A party may denounce the Convention or any Protocol by notice to the Depositary; denunciation takes effect after one year, but a party then engaged in an armed conflict or occupation covered by Art. 1 remains bound until it ends and protected persons have been released or repatriated. Denouncing the Convention denounces every Protocol the party has accepted (Art. 9).
- **Amendment.** Any party may propose amendments to the Convention or to a Protocol it is bound by; the Depositary convenes a conference if a majority of parties, not fewer than eighteen, agree. Amendments enter into force in the same way as the Convention, and amendments to a Protocol may be adopted only by its parties (Art. 8(1)). The text states no voting rule; conferences have worked by consensus.
- **Review.** If no conference has been held within ten years of entry into force, any party may request a review conference (Art. 8(3)). Since 1995 the parties have in practice convened one about every five years.
- **Depositary and texts.** The UN Secretary-General is Depositary (Art. 10); the Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish texts are equally authentic (Art. 11).

## History and compliance

### Origins and negotiation
Weapon-specific restrictions were left unresolved by the 1974-77 Diplomatic Conference that produced the Additional Protocols to the Geneva Conventions, after preparatory ICRC expert conferences at Lucerne (1974) and Lugano (1976). A United Nations conference met in Geneva from 10 to 28 September 1979 and from 15 September to 10 October 1980. On 10 October 1980 it adopted the Convention with Protocols I (non-detectable fragments), II (mines and booby-traps) and III (incendiary weapons). The Convention entered into force on 2 December 1983 and was registered as UNTS No. 22495; Trinidad and Tobago (29 July 2024) is the most recent accession.

### Review conferences and development
- **First (Vienna, 25 September to 13 October 1995; Geneva, January and April-May 1996):** adopted Protocol IV on blinding lasers and Amended Protocol II.
- **Second (Geneva, 11-21 December 2001):** amended Art. 1 to cover non-international armed conflicts (in force 18 May 2004) and launched the expert work that produced Protocol V, adopted by the Meeting of States Parties on 28 November 2003.
- **Third (Geneva, 7-17 November 2006):** adopted the compliance mechanism, a universalization plan and a sponsorship programme. An Implementation Support Unit followed in November 2009.
- **Fourth (Geneva, 14-25 November 2011):** did not reach consensus on a draft Protocol VI on cluster munitions.
- **Fifth (Geneva, 12-16 December 2016):** established a Group of Governmental Experts on lethal autonomous weapons systems.
- **Sixth (Geneva, 13-17 December 2021):** considered the continuation of that work.
- **Seventh:** scheduled for 16-20 November 2026 in Geneva, after a Preparatory Committee on 7-9 September 2026.

### Compliance and operation
The framework text has no verification or enforcement machinery, and no formal compliance finding under it was identified in the sources reviewed. Allegations about particular weapons, such as incendiary weapons or mines, concern the Protocols and are recorded there. The main operational strain has been financial: the Arms Control Association reported in July 2017 that unpaid assessments, most notably by Brazil, had forced the cancellation of meetings.

### Coding notes
- **Weapon families and provisions.** Left empty, and only two provision rows are recorded (dissemination under Art. 6 and the two-Protocol requirement under Art. 4(3)), because the framework restricts no weapon. Restrictions are coded on the Protocol records.
- **Review conferences.** Coded "Ad hoc / on request" because Art. 8(3) only allows a conference on request after ten years; the five-year rhythm since 1995 is practice. The count of 6 excludes the Seventh Review Conference, which had not met at the time of coding.
- **Duration and amendment.** Duration is "Not specified" because the text has no term clause. Amendment is "Other" because Art. 8(1) requires a majority of at least eighteen parties to convene a conference but states no voting rule.
- **Third-party roles.** Coded "Yes" because Art. 7(2) and (4) let non-parties, and authorities of the type described in Additional Protocol I Art. 96(3), accept and apply the Convention, and Art. 8 gives the Depositary the function of convening amendment and review conferences.
- **Reporting, enforcement and dispute resolution.** Coded "Not specified in text". The 2006 reporting arrangement is a political decision of the parties, not a treaty obligation.
- **Word count.** About 2,420 words for the title, preamble and Arts. 1-11 (about 1,955 for the articles alone), counted from the text recognized on the scanned certified copy; approximate to about 1 percent.

- Treaty Name (Full) reads 'CCW Framework Convention'; the official title is 'Convention on Prohibitions or Restrictions on the Use of Certain Conventional Weapons which may be deemed to be Excessively Injurious or to have Indiscriminate Effects'. The page body's 'Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons' is the common name, not the official one.
- Number of Parties is 126; the UN Treaty Collection lists 128 as of 11 October 2026 (recent accessions include Malawi 2022, Singapore 2023, Trinidad and Tobago 2024).
- Number of Signatories is 51; the UN Treaty Collection lists 50.
- Date Entered into Force is empty; it is 2 December 1983 (Art. 5(1)).
- Signed at is empty; signature was at UN Headquarters, New York (Art. 3). Depositary (UN Secretary-General) is empty.
- The page body's entry-into-force note ('must sign on to at least two of the convention's protocols') misstates Art. 4(3): states must notify consent to be bound by at least two Protocols when they ratify or accede; it is a condition of joining, not an entry-into-force requirement for the Convention, which entered into force six months after the twentieth instrument (Art. 5(1)).
- Treaty URL points to a UNODA overview page, not the official text; replaced with the UNTC certified true copy.
- Verification, enforcement and dispute resolution are recorded as 'None'. The text is silent ('Not specified in text'); the 2006 compliance mechanism decision adds consultation and national reporting outside the treaty text.
- Type tag 'Conventional' is acceptable, but the subject matter is conduct of hostilities (IHL); the framework itself limits no specific weapon.
