# Page body

## Overview
The INF Treaty required the United States and the Soviet Union to eliminate all of their ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres, together with their launchers, support structures and support equipment, and banned the future production and flight-testing of such missiles. It was the first agreement to eliminate an entire category of US and Soviet nuclear delivery systems, and it relied on extensive on-site inspection. The two sides eliminated 2,692 missiles by May 1991. From 2014 the United States alleged that Russia had developed a prohibited ground-launched cruise missile, which Russia denied; the United States withdrew, and the Treaty terminated on 2 August 2019.

## Provisions and obligations

### Core obligations
- **Elimination of intermediate-range missiles (over 1,000 and up to 5,500 km).** All missiles, launchers, support structures and support equipment to be eliminated within three years (Art. IV(1)). Phase-one ceilings at 29 months capped deployed launchers at 171 warhead-equivalents, deployed missiles at 180 warheads, and aggregate launchers and missiles at 200 warheads each, and froze the GLBM share at its 1 November 1987 level (Art. IV(2)(a)).
- **Elimination of shorter-range missiles (500 to 1,000 km).** All to be eliminated within 18 months (Art. V(1)); deployed missiles and all launchers moved to elimination facilities within 90 days and non-deployed missiles within 12 months, with missile and launcher facilities at least 1,000 km apart (Art. V(2)-(3)).
- **Permanent bans.** Neither Party may produce or flight-test intermediate-range missiles or produce their stages or launchers (Art. VI(1)(a)), or produce, flight-test or launch shorter-range missiles or produce their stages or launchers (Art. VI(1)(b)); neither Party may "have such systems thereafter" (Art. I).
- **Scope.** The bans apply to ground-launched systems regardless of warhead (Art. II). Exceptions cover missile-defence interceptors (Art. VII(3)), sea- or air-mode missiles tested from fixed test launchers (Art. VII(11)), R&D boosters with at most 35 launchers (Art. VII(12)), and one permitted GLBM type with an outwardly similar stage (Art. VI(2)).

### Locational and operational limits
- Missiles and launchers only in deployment areas, at support facilities or in transit (Art. VIII(1)-(3)); transit within 25 days (Art. VIII(4)); no increase in number, or change in location or boundaries, of the declared deployment areas, operating bases and support facilities, except elimination facilities (Art. VIII(5)); from 30 days after entry into force, none at listed missile or launcher production facilities or test ranges (Art. VIII(6)); none at training facilities (Art. VIII(7)).
- Removal for elimination in complete units: Pershing II batteries, BGM-109G flights, SS-20 regiments (Art. X(3)). Up to 100 intermediate-range missiles could be eliminated by launching in the first six months (Art. X(5)). Pershing IB and SSC-X-4 eliminated within six months (Art. X(6)). Deployment areas, bases and support facilities to be eliminated (Art. X(8)).

### Transparency and verification
- **Data and notifications.** MOU data base as of 1 November 1987, updated within 30 days of entry into force and every six months, plus advance and 48-hour notifications, all through the Nuclear Risk Reduction Centers (Art. IX, XIII(2)).
- **On-site inspection.** Baseline inspections to verify the initial data, completed within 90 days of entry into force (Art. XI(3)); closeout inspections within 60 days of a facility's scheduled elimination (Art. XI(4)); and inspections of the elimination process and its completion (Art. XI(7)-(8)). For 13 years, short-notice inspections at 20, 15 and then ten per year, including in basing countries (Art. XI(5)), and continuous portal monitoring at Votkinsk and Magna, Utah (Art. XI(6)).
- **National technical means.** No interference with NTM and no concealment impeding verification (Art. XII(1)-(2)); up to six requests a year for open display of road-mobile ICBMs during the first three years (Art. XII(3)).
- **Special Verification Commission.** A standing bilateral body to resolve compliance questions and agree measures to improve the Treaty's viability and effectiveness (Art. XIII(1)).

### Protocols and memorandum
- **Protocol on Elimination.** Specifies destruction methods for each item (Sec. II(10)); stages burned must not be instrumented (Sec. II(7)); elimination launches must be single, six hours apart, not used as interceptor targets, and without telemetry beyond range-safety data (Sec. III); static display capped at 15 missiles, 15 canisters and 15 launchers (Sec. V(2)); accidental losses notified within 48 hours (Sec. V(1)).
- **Protocol on Inspection.** Inspector and aircrew lists capped at 200; teams of ten, 20 or 30; 16-hour or 72-hour notice; 24-hour inspection periods; portal perimeter, declaration and measurement rules (Sec. III-IX).
- **Memorandum of Understanding.** Declared 695 US and 826 Soviet intermediate-range missiles, 170 US and 926 Soviet shorter-range missiles, and listed facilities and support items (Sec. II).

## Participation and legal operation
The Treaty bound only the United States and the Soviet Union. It entered into force on 1 June 1988, when instruments of ratification were exchanged at Moscow (Art. XVII(1)); the US Senate had given its advice and consent on 27 May 1988 by 93 votes to 5. It designates no depositary; both Parties registered it with the UN Secretariat on 13 December 1991 (Art. XVII(2)). The NATO basing countries (Belgium, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom) were not parties but accepted inspections on their territory. After the Soviet Union dissolved, implementation was extended to the six successor states with inspectable facilities (Belarus, Kazakhstan, Russia, Turkmenistan, Ukraine and Uzbekistan), of which Belarus, Kazakhstan, Russia and Ukraine participated actively.
- **Duration.** Unlimited (Art. XV(1)), but the inspection rights in Art. XI(5)-(6) ran for 13 years only and ended on 31 May 2001.
- **Withdrawal.** A Party may withdraw if it decides that extraordinary events related to the subject matter of the Treaty have jeopardized its supreme interests, giving six months' notice with a statement of those events (Art. XV(2)). The United States invoked this clause on 2 February 2019.
- **Amendment.** Either Party may propose amendments, which enter into force only when both Parties ratify them (Art. XVI, XVII). Measures the Parties agree in the Special Verification Commission to improve the viability and effectiveness of the Protocol on Elimination or the Protocol on Inspection are not deemed amendments to the Treaty (final sections of each Protocol, under Art. XIII(1)(b)).
- **Review.** There are no review conferences; consultation runs through the Special Verification Commission.
- **Relationship to other obligations.** Neither Party may assume international obligations that conflict with the Treaty (Art. XIV).

## History and compliance

### Origins and negotiation
Soviet deployment of the SS-20 from the mid-1970s led NATO to adopt its 1979 dual-track decision, pairing planned Pershing II and ground-launched cruise missile deployments with an offer to negotiate. Talks stalled while US deployments went ahead in the early 1980s. After 1985 the negotiations broadened to a global ban, and Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev signed the Treaty at Washington on 8 December 1987.

### Implementation
The State Department reports that the United States eliminated its last covered missiles in late April and early May 1991 and the USSR its last declared SS-20 in May 1991, a total of 2,692 missiles. Inspection and monitoring ended on 31 May 2001 under Art. XI. On 25 October 2007 the United States and Russia jointly called at the UN General Assembly for other states to renounce INF-range missiles.

### Compliance dispute, 2014-2019
- **Allegation.** In its July 2014 compliance report, confirmed publicly on 29 July 2014, the United States alleged that Russia was violating its obligations not to possess, produce or flight-test a GLCM with a range of 500 to 5,500 km, or to possess or produce its launchers. Later reports repeated the allegation, and in late 2017 the United States identified the missile as the 9M729 (SSC-8).
- **Response and counter-allegations.** Russia denied the allegation and alleged US noncompliance: Aegis Ashore Mk-41 launchers in Europe, target missiles for missile-defence tests, and armed drones. The United States rejected these claims.
- **Consultation.** The Special Verification Commission met in November 2016 and in December 2017 without resolving the dispute.
- **Finding of material breach.** On 4 December 2018 the United States declared Russia in material breach and set a 60-day deadline. NATO foreign ministers stated that Russia had violated the Treaty. On 23 January 2019 Russia displayed the 9M729 canister and maintained that its range was below 500 km.
- **Suspension and withdrawal.** On 2 February 2019 the United States suspended its obligations and gave notice of withdrawal under Art. XV(2). Russia announced its own suspension the same day, formalized by decree on 4 March 2019. The US withdrawal took effect on 2 August 2019 and the Treaty ceased to operate. The State Department said Russia was solely responsible; Russia rejected that view.
- **Aftermath.** On 4 August 2025 the Russian Foreign Ministry stated that Russia no longer considered itself bound by its self-declared moratorium on deploying INF-range missiles.

### Coding notes
- **Weapon families.** Coded as missiles and delivery systems, plus fortifications and bases for the deployment areas and support facilities the Treaty required to be eliminated. Nuclear weapons are not coded: the Treaty covers the missiles regardless of warhead and does not limit warheads, which could be removed before elimination (Protocol on Elimination Sec. II(3)).
- **Number of parties.** Counted as 2 as of entry into force on 1 June 1988: the United States and the USSR. The count is not updated for Soviet succession. After December 1991 implementation was extended to the six successor states with inspectable facilities through separate arrangements rather than accession, and the sources disagree on the formal party count at termination: the State Department refers to six successor states with facilities, four of them active, while CRS refers to Russia and 11 other successor states. Successor-state participation is described under Participation and legal operation.
- **Amendment procedure.** Coded "Unanimity" because amendments require the agreement and ratification of both Parties.
- **National authority.** Coded "Yes" under the codebook definition: the instrument requires each party to designate or use a national body, focal point or centre for implementation or communication. Art. IX(2) and XIII(2) require each Party to provide data updates, notifications and inspection notices through its Nuclear Risk Reduction Center, established under the US-Soviet agreement of 15 September 1987. The Treaty does not create a national implementing authority with domestic regulatory functions of the kind required by the Chemical Weapons Convention.
- **Word count.** 6,283 words counts the English treaty text only (17 articles). The Memorandum of Understanding, both protocols and the annex are integral parts of the Treaty (Art. I, XVII) but are excluded; with them the English text in UNTS vol. 1657 runs to roughly 33,000 words.
- **Undated events.** No single date was found for the extension of implementation to the Soviet successor states, for the November 2016 Special Verification Commission session, or for Soviet ratification; those event rows carry no date or only the exchange of instruments.

## Correction to earlier entry: INF: corrections to the earlier entry

- Treaty Name (Full) gave the common name 'Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (INF)'; the official title is 'Treaty between the United States of America and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics on the Elimination of Their Intermediate-Range and Shorter-Range Missiles'.
- 'Date Ratified' and 'Signatories & Dates' give 27 May 1988 for both Parties. That is the date of US Senate advice and consent (93-5), not ratification by either Party; instruments of ratification were exchanged at Moscow on 1 June 1988.
- Depository listed as 'US, USSR'. The bilateral Treaty designates no depositary; it was registered with the UN Secretariat by both Parties on 13 December 1991 (Art. XVII(2), UNTS No. 28521).
- The body field 'Extension provisions' quotes the Art. XV withdrawal clause; the Treaty has no extension provision because its duration is unlimited.
- Word count '8500' has no stated basis; the English treaty text (17 articles, excluding MOU and protocols) is about 6,283 words.
- 'Type of Limitation: Possession, stockpiling' is incomplete; the Treaty also bans production and flight-testing (Art. VI) and requires elimination (Art. IV-V, X), with locational limits (Art. VIII).
- Weapon types listed 'Nuclear'. The Treaty limits ground-launched missiles regardless of warhead and does not limit nuclear warheads themselves (warhead devices may be removed before elimination, Protocol on Elimination Sec. II(3)).
- 'Compliance History: Bad' is an unattributed verdict; replaced with attributed events (US allegations from 2014, Russian denials and counter-allegations).
- Verification recorded as 'On site inspections' only; the Treaty also provides national technical means, non-interference, continuous portal monitoring and cooperative measures (Art. XI-XII).
- Treaty URL was blank; the official text is UNTS vol. 1657, No. 28521.
- Status 'Terminated' confirmed: US withdrawal effective 2 August 2019; Expiration/end date was blank.
