# Page body

## Overview
Protocol IV to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons prohibits the use of laser weapons specifically designed to cause permanent blindness, and their transfer to any state or non-state entity. Adopted in 1995 at the First CCW Review Conference, it banned a weapon category before it had been used on the battlefield, which the ICRC described in 1998 as only the second such prohibition in history, after the 1868 St Petersburg Declaration. It does not ban research, development, production or stockpiling, and it excludes blinding that occurs incidentally to the legitimate use of other laser systems. It has 111 parties, including all five permanent members of the Security Council.

## Provisions and obligations

### Core prohibitions
- **Prohibition of use (Art. 1).** Parties may not employ laser weapons specifically designed, as their sole combat function or as one of their combat functions, to cause permanent blindness to unenhanced vision, meaning the naked eye or the eye with corrective eyesight devices.
- **Prohibition of transfer (Art. 1).** Parties shall not transfer such weapons to any state or non-state entity.
- **What is not prohibited.** The Protocol does not prohibit research, development, production or stockpiling, and contains no destruction obligation.

### Precautions, exclusions and definition
- **Precautions (Art. 2).** In employing any laser system, parties must take all feasible precautions to avoid permanent blindness to unenhanced vision, including training of armed forces and other practical measures.
- **Incidental blinding excluded (Art. 3).** Blinding as an incidental or collateral effect of the legitimate military employment of laser systems, including lasers used against optical equipment, is outside the prohibition.
- **Definition (Art. 4).** Permanent blindness is irreversible and uncorrectable loss of vision that is seriously disabling with no prospect of recovery, equivalent to visual acuity below 20/200 Snellen measured using both eyes.

### Field of application
- The Protocol applies in the situations covered by CCW Art. 1: international armed conflicts, extended to non-international armed conflicts for parties bound by the 2001 amendment to Art. 1.
- Australia, Belgium, Ireland, the Netherlands, Sweden, the United Kingdom and others declared on consent that they will apply the Protocol in all circumstances or at all times.
- The United States filed an understanding that decisions under Art. 2 are judged on the information reasonably available at the time.
- The Protocol contains no reporting, inspection, verification or dispute-settlement provisions.

## Participation and legal operation
The Protocol is an annex to the CCW and forms an integral part of the Convention for each state bound by it (CCW Art. 4(5)). It was never opened for signature: only CCW parties can become bound, by notifying the Depositary of their consent (CCW Art. 4(3)-(4)). Its own text has two articles in the Additional Protocol (annexation and entry into force) and four in Protocol IV; its final clauses all come from the Convention.
- **Entry into force.** Six months after twenty states had notified consent, and for each later state six months after its notification (Additional Protocol Art. 2; CCW Art. 5(3)-(4)). Hungary's notification of 30 January 1998 was the twentieth, and the Protocol entered into force on 30 July 1998.
- **Duration and withdrawal.** No fixed term. A party may denounce the Protocol by notice to the Depositary; denunciation takes effect after one year, but a party then engaged in an armed conflict or occupation covered by CCW Art. 1 remains bound until it ends (CCW Art. 9).
- **Amendment.** Proposed through the Depositary; a conference is convened if a majority of parties, not fewer than eighteen, agree, and amendments to the Protocol may be adopted only by the parties bound by it (CCW Art. 8(1)). The text states no voting rule; CCW conferences work by consensus in practice.
- **Review.** The Protocol has no review mechanism of its own; its operation is considered at CCW review conferences (CCW Art. 8(3)).
- **Depositary.** The UN Secretary-General (CCW Art. 10).

## History and compliance

### Origins
Sweden brought blinding laser weapons to international attention, circulating a position paper in 1987 and later proposing a protocol during the preparations for the First CCW Review Conference. The ICRC convened four expert meetings between June 1989 and April 1991. According to Human Rights Watch Arms Project findings cited by the ICRC's Louise Doswald-Beck, research on such weapons was under way in China, France, Germany, Israel, Russia and the United Kingdom, alongside ten active or dormant US programmes ([Doswald-Beck, International Review of the Red Cross, 1996](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-the-red-cross-1961-1997/article/new-protocol-on-blinding-laser-weapons/B72BF4A7260BC6B957EF6786A5D79CFE)).

### Adoption and participation
Protocol IV was adopted on 13 October 1995 at the Vienna session of the First Review Conference, under CCW Art. 8(3)(b). Finland was the first state to notify consent (11 January 1996). The United States notified consent on 21 January 2009, with an understanding on Art. 2. As of 11 October 2026 the UN Treaty Collection lists 111 parties; the most recent are Singapore (2023) and Trinidad and Tobago (2024). States not bound include Egypt, Indonesia, Iran, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, the Republic of Korea, Syria and Viet Nam. The ICRC customary IHL study (Rule 86) treats the prohibition of use as customary law.

### Compliance
No formal allegation of a violation of Protocol IV, and no denunciation, was identified in the sources reviewed. In May 2018 the US Department of Defense stated that lasers from China's base in Djibouti had caused minor eye injuries to two US C-130 pilots and lodged a demarche; China's Foreign Ministry said the accusations were inconsistent with the facts. That exchange concerned laser harassment and was not framed by either side as a Protocol IV matter.

### Coding notes
- **Signature fields.** Left blank because the Protocol was never opened for signature; a blank here means no signature stage existed, not a missing value.
- **Restriction types.** Coded as prohibitions of use and transfer only. The precautions duty in Art. 2 is coded "Other" because it regulates the use of lawful laser systems rather than banning an activity.
- **Final-clause fields.** Duration (indefinite), withdrawal (12 months' notice), amendment and review are coded from the CCW articles that govern the Protocol, as each clause field states. Amendment is coded "Other" because CCW Art. 8 sets no voting rule.
- **Review conferences held.** The count of 5 covers the CCW review conferences held after the Protocol's adoption (2001, 2006, 2011, 2016, 2021); the Protocol has none of its own.
- **Effective dates.** The effective dates for the United States (21 July 2009) and Trinidad and Tobago (29 January 2025) are calculated as six months after notification under CCW Art. 5(4), not read from the depositary record.
- **Djibouti laser incident.** Recorded as "Other" rather than as a compliance allegation, because neither government framed it as a Protocol IV matter.
- **Size.** 6 articles and 276 words cover the full English instrument (two Additional Protocol articles and four Protocol articles); the operative Arts. 1-4 alone are 166 words.

## Correction to earlier entry: CCW Protocol IV: corrections to the earlier entry

- Type of Limitation lists Use, Development, Production, Stockpiling, Transfer, Testing and Deployment, and the page body says 'Complete prohibition, so all of the above'. The text prohibits only use and transfer (Art. 1) and requires precautions (Art. 2); development, production, stockpiling, testing and deployment are not restricted.
- Quantitative Limits 'Zero (Complete Ban)' / body 'Yes - 0' is inaccurate: the Protocol sets no numerical limit and is not a complete ban.
- Number of Signatories 159 is wrong: the Protocol was never opened for signature. The body's '# of Parties 159' is also wrong.
- Number of Parties 109 is outdated: the UN Treaty Collection lists 111 parties as of 11 October 2026 (the page's own table already has 111 rows).
- The 'Signatories & Dates' and 'Date Signed' fields contain dates of consent to be bound, not signatures.
- Bosnia and Herzegovina's date is 11 October 2001 per UNTC, not 10 October 2001.
- Uruguay's date is 18 September 1998 per UNTC, not 18 August 1998.
- Montenegro became bound by succession (23 October 2006), not ratification.
- Official name on the page ('Protocol on Blinding Laser Weapons (Protocol IV)') is the annexed protocol's title; the depositary title is 'Additional Protocol to the Convention ... (Protocol IV, entitled Protocol on Blinding Laser Weapons)'.
- Enforcement Mechanisms 'Yes' and Dispute Resolution 'Yes' are not supported by the text; neither the Protocol nor the CCW contains enforcement or dispute-settlement clauses.
- Reservations 'Allowed' is not supported: neither the Protocol nor the CCW addresses reservations. Parties filed declarations and one understanding (United States).
- Word count 174 / article count 4 understate the instrument: the English Additional Protocol has 6 articles and 276 words (166 words in the operative body of Protocol IV Arts. 1-4).
- Entry into force requirement '20 Ratifications' should read twenty notifications of consent to be bound, with entry into force six months later (CCW Art. 5(3)).
- Extension provisions 'None' and Review Conference 'In Parent Treaty' are acceptable in substance but the duration, withdrawal and amendment rules should be stated as derived from CCW Arts. 8-9.
- Compliance History 'Strong' is an unattributed verdict and should be replaced with attributed events.
- For US President 'Bill Clinton' reflects the adoption date; US consent to be bound was notified on 21 January 2009.
