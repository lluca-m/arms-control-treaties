# Page body

## Overview
The Rush-Bagot Agreement is an exchange of notes of 28 and 29 April 1817 between the British Minister in Washington, Charles Bagot, and Acting Secretary of State Richard Rush. In its original terms it confined the naval force each side might keep on Lake Ontario, the Upper Lakes and Lake Champlain to four small vessels in all, each of no more than 100 tons and armed with one 18-pound cannon, required all other armed vessels there to be dismantled, and barred building or arming further vessels of war on the Lakes. Concluded by the United Kingdom, it is now applied between Canada and the United States, and the US Treaties in Force (as of 1 January 2025) lists it as in force. It remains in force as subsequently interpreted. In 1939 the United States described its technical provisions as obsolete and Canada agreed that its technical scheme was somewhat out of date; since then the two governments have adapted it by agreed interpretations rather than amendment.

## Provisions and obligations
The instrument has no numbered articles. Citations refer to paragraphs of the [Bagot note of 28 April 1817 and the Rush note of 29 April 1817](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101662&lang=eng), which state the same terms.

### Original 1817 limits
- On Lake Ontario, each side may keep one vessel (Bagot note, para. 3; Rush note, para. 3).
- On the Upper Lakes, each side may keep two vessels (Bagot note, para. 4; Rush note, para. 3). The notes do not define the Upper Lakes; the force statements exchanged in November 1816 reported Lakes Erie and Huron under this head ([Hunter Miller, notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817n.asp)).
- On the waters of Lake Champlain, each side may keep one vessel (Bagot note, para. 5; Rush note, para. 3).
- Each permitted vessel may not exceed 100 tons burthen and may carry one 18-pound cannon (Bagot note, paras. 3 to 5; Rush note, para. 3).

### Dismantling and construction
- All other armed vessels on the Lakes are to be dismantled "forthwith" (Bagot note, para. 6; Rush note, para. 4). No numerical period is specified; dismantling was required forthwith. No verification procedure is stated.
- No other vessels of war are to be built or armed on the Lakes (same paragraphs).

### Employment of the permitted vessels
- Each government undertakes to order that its limited force be restricted to services that do not interfere with the proper duties of the other party's armed vessels (Bagot note, para. 8; Rush note, para. 5).

### Bound parties and scope
- The obligations bind both parties identically ("on each side"). They apply only to the named waters and do not address land forces, fortifications or naval forces elsewhere.
- The notes contain no provisions on information exchange, inspection, consultation, dispute settlement or enforcement.

### Later interpretations
The 1817 text has never been amended. Canada and the United States have instead recorded agreed interpretations by four exchanges of notes, which the parties described as consistent with the agreement's underlying objective though not strictly with its technical scheme. The detailed limits above are coded against the original text; these exchanges explain how the agreement has operated since.
- **1939 (9 and 10 June, Ottawa).** The US Minister, Daniel Roper, proposed and Canada's Under-Secretary for External Affairs, O. D. Skelton, accepted: free movement of naval vessels within the Lakes; reserve training and target practice in each party's own waters; two 4-inch guns on each of three US vessels, dismantled outside the summer training season; and building in Lakes shipyards of naval vessels for ocean service, with advance information, removal on completion and no armament until the seaboard. Canada stated its assumption that these understandings applied equally to Canadian vessels and shipyards; the United States did not separately confirm this. ([Supporting record](https://www.notion.so/3f72b9a41a30811fb40dca6bf3dd9277); [text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101663&lang=eng); [UNTS vol. 149, No. 476](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20149/v149.pdf#page=356))
- **1940 (30 October and 2 November, Ottawa).** Skelton proposed and the US Minister, Pierrepont Moffat, accepted that armament could be installed on vessels built on the Lakes if it was incapable of immediate use while they remained there. The notes also record that new practical measures were to be settled by consultation and correspondence. ([text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101664&lang=eng))
- **1942 (26 February and 9 March, Ottawa).** Moffat proposed and Canada agreed that vessels built on the Lakes might have their armament placed in complete readiness and undergo all essential tests and trials there, including submerged operation of submarines, for the duration of the hostilities only. ([text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101666&lang=eng))
- **1946 (18 November and 5 or 6 December, Washington).** Canada's Ambassador, Hume Wrong, proposed and Acting Secretary of State Dean Acheson accepted that either government may station naval vessels on the Lakes to train naval reserves, provided full information on their number, disposition, functions and armament is given to the other in advance; the Canadian note records both governments' view that the agreement's detailed provisions no longer fit present conditions and that its spirit guides them. The official texts differ on the date of the US reply: Canada's Treaty Series prints 5 December 1946, the UN Treaty Series 6 December 1946, which is also the entry-into-force date given there and in the US Treaties in Force. ([text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101665&lang=eng))

## Participation and legal operation
- **Form and parties.** A bilateral exchange of notes signed at Washington on 28 April 1817 (Bagot, for the United Kingdom) and 29 April 1817 (Rush, for the United States). The US [Treaties in Force](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Treaties-in-Force-2025-FINAL.pdf) (as of 1 January 2025, Canada, p. 62) now lists it under Canada, and [Canada's Treaty Information](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/details.aspx?id=101662&lang=eng) lists it with the United States as the other party. The sources reviewed do not state when or by what act Canada assumed the British rights and obligations.
- **Entry into force.** The notes set no condition. Both governments' records give 29 April 1817, the date of the second note. The United States subsequently submitted the arrangement to the Senate, which approved it on 16 April 1818, and the President proclaimed it on 28 April 1818 ([Hunter Miller, notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817n.asp)).
- **Duration.** No term is stated; the limits apply "henceforth" (Bagot note, para. 2; Rush note, para. 2).
- **Termination.** Either party may annul the stipulation by notice; it then ceases to bind six months after the date of the notice (Bagot note, para. 7; Rush note, para. 4).
- **Amendment and review.** No amendment or review procedure is stated. The parties have instead recorded interpretations by further exchanges of notes (1939, 1940, 1942, 1946).
- **Depositary.** None; the original notes are held in the two governments' files.
- **Reservations.** Not addressed; the Senate resolution of 16 April 1818 attached no conditions ([text](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817r.asp)).

## History and compliance

### Origins
After the War of 1812, in which both sides built fleets on the Lakes, the United States proposed reciprocal limits. The Office of the Historian dates John Quincy Adams's proposal in London to 25 January 1816 ([Milestones](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1801-1829/rush-bagot)). In August 1816 Secretary of State Monroe gave Bagot a precise project; Bagot lacked authority to agree details but suspended further augmentation of British forces, and the two governments exchanged statements of their forces in November 1816 ([Hunter Miller, notes and correspondence](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817n.asp)). The British acceptance (28 April 1817) and the US confirmation (29 April 1817) completed the agreement ([text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101662&lang=eng)).

### Implementation and US approval
Hunter Miller records that the agreement was put into operation immediately on both sides, with US Navy orders dated 2 May 1817. President Monroe submitted it to the Senate on 6 April 1818, asking whether it required advice and consent; the Senate approved it on 16 April 1818, and Monroe proclaimed it on 28 April 1818 ([notes](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817n.asp); [proclamation](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/br1817p.asp)).

### Civil War notice of termination, withdrawn before taking effect
- **Notice.** The United States gave notice of termination by a communication that took effect on 23 November 1864. A joint resolution of Congress approved on 9 February 1865 adopted and ratified the notice, stating that hostile expeditions against Lakes commerce endangered the frontier and that the naval force allowed by the agreement might be insufficient to prevent them ([Joint Resolution No. 13, 13 Stat. 568](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/13/567); [FRUS 1865, I, doc. 85](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p1/d85)).
- **US position.** On 8 March 1865 Secretary Seward instructed Minister Adams to say that the United States was willing for the agreement to remain practically in force and had built or armed no additional vessels on the Lakes ([FRUS 1865, I, doc. 110](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p1/d110)).
- **British view.** In the House of Lords on 23 March 1865, Earl Russell said Confederate seizures of vessels on the Lakes justified the notice, as reported by The Times ([FRUS 1865, I, doc. 138](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p1/d138)).
- **Withdrawal.** After a British request for clarification, Seward confirmed on 16 June 1865 that the notice was withdrawn within the six months, and on 22 August 1865 accepted the British understanding that the agreement continues in force unless terminated by fresh notice ([FRUS 1865, II, doc. 285](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p2/d285); [doc. 286](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p2/d286); [doc. 315](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p2/d315)).

### Twentieth-century interpretations
In June 1939 the United States stated that the technical provisions had long been obsolete, that there was a clear record of what may have been considered technical violations by both parties, and that the US vessels then on the Lakes might not be entirely in keeping with a literal reading; Canada accepted the proposed interpretations, stating its assumption that they would apply equally to Canadian vessels and shipyards ([1939 exchange](https://app.notion.com/p/3f72b9a41a30811fb40dca6bf3dd9277); [text](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101663&lang=eng)). Further interpretations followed in [1940](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101664&lang=eng), [1942](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101666&lang=eng) and [1946](https://www.treaty-accord.gc.ca/text-texte.aspx?id=101665&lang=eng), the last recording that the spirit rather than the detailed provisions guides the two governments. No formal finding of non-compliance by either government was identified in the sources reviewed. The agreement remains listed as in force in the US [Treaties in Force](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Treaties-in-Force-2025-FINAL.pdf) (2025).

### Coding notes
- **Form.** Coded as an exchange of notes: a British note of 28 April 1817 and a US note of 29 April 1817 stating identical terms. It is often called the Rush-Bagot Treaty, and the US Senate approved it by the two-thirds procedure in 1818, but its form is an exchange of notes.
- **Dates.** The single adoption and signature date carries 29 April 1817, when the second note completed the exchange; the first note was signed on 28 April 1817. Entry into force is 29 April 1817 in both the US and Canadian treaty records; the 1818 Senate approval and proclamation came after the agreement was already in operation and are recorded as events. The Avalon Project's header dates Bagot's note 16 April 1817; the note itself, Rush's reply and Canada's official text give 28 April.
- **Parties.** Two, counted as of 1 January 2025 (the Treaties in Force edition consulted). The original parties were the United Kingdom and the United States; Canada is counted in place of the United Kingdom because both governments' official treaty records now list the agreement as between Canada and the United States. When and how Canada succeeded was not established.
- **Operating environment and subject matter.** Coded Sea as the naval domain, although the waters concerned are inland lakes. Subject matter is coded as naval arms limitation: the instrument caps and reduces naval forces in named waters and restricts their employment; it does not establish a general demilitarized zone.
- **Yes/No fields.** All six are coded "Not specified in text", from the instrument's own text. The notes record that each government issued or would issue orders to its own naval officers (Bagot note, para. 8; Rush note, para. 5), which does not amount to designating a national authority. No enforcement mechanism is specified; either party may terminate on six months' notice.
- **Information and inspection.** Coded from the original 1817 text, which has none. Advance information was introduced by the later interpretive exchanges (1939 and 1940 for construction; 1946 for training vessels), which have no rows of their own in the database yet.
- **1946 date.** The official texts differ on the date of the 1946 interpretation: Canada's Treaty Series text prints the US note as dated 5 December 1946, while the UN Treaty Series (vol. 149) prints it as 6 December 1946, and the UN Treaty Series and the US Treaties in Force 2025 give entry into force on 6 December 1946. The event is shown as "5 or 6 December 1946".
- **Amendment.** None stated. The 1939 to 1946 exchanges are recorded as interpretations, not amendments, because the parties described them that way.
- **Events.** The 1864 notice is coded as a withdrawal notice: it never took effect because the United States withdrew it within the six months. The twentieth-century exchanges are coded "Other" because they interpret rather than amend the agreement. The 1939 US statement about possible technical violations is not coded as an acknowledged breach because it is expressly qualified.
- **Provision rows.** One row per body of water, because each sets a different number of vessels; the 100-ton and one-gun limits apply to every permitted vessel and have rows of their own; building and arming further vessels of war are separate rows because they restrict different activities. Every obligation binds both parties identically.
- **Size.** 787 words across the two notes (bodies and signatures, Canadian text); the notes have nine and six paragraphs and no articles.

## Research note: Rush-Bagot: research notes

    - The local PDF is the Avalon Project reproduction of Hunter Miller's text. Its header misdates the Bagot note as "WASHINGTON April 16, 1817"; Hunter Miller's own notes, the Rush note ("note of the 28th of this month") and Canada's official text all give 28 April 1817. treaty_url uses Canada's official text instead.
    - state.gov returned HTTP 403 to direct requests; the Treaties in Force 2025 PDF was read from the Wayback Machine copy: https://web.archive.org/web/20260106032927/https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Treaties-in-Force-2025-FINAL.pdf
    - The 1946 exchange: Canada Treaty Information and the text of the US note give 5 December 1946; US Treaties in Force 2025 (p. 63) gives 'November 18 and December 6, 1946' and entry into force 6 December 1946. The event uses the note's own date and records the discrepancy.
    - The 1939 exchange appears in Treaties in Force 2025 as 'Agreement relating to the construction of naval vessels on the Great Lakes' and the 1940 exchange as 'Agreement relating to the armament of naval vessels to be incapable of immediate use'; the 1942 wartime exchange is not listed (it applied for the duration of hostilities only).
    - Not verified and therefore omitted: reports of a 2004 to 2006 US-Canadian understanding allowing US Coast Guard vessels on the Lakes to mount machine guns as law-enforcement weapons (found only in Wikipedia, press and forum material; no official text retrieved, and no such agreement appears in Treaties in Force 2025); British naval build-up on the Lakes during the 1837 to 1841 Canadian rebellions; the 1864 US notifications of a temporary increase of its force (Seward to Burnley, September 1864, FRUS 1864 Part II), which history.state.gov rate-limited before retrieval.
    - The exact date of Adams's March 1865 note to Russell was not found. Seward's letter of 16 June 1865 refers to 'a subsequent note of the 8th of March' by Adams, but 8 March is the date of Seward's instruction in Washington (FRUS 1865 I, doc. 110), and Bruce's note says Adams acted 'in compliance with instructions, dated March 8th'.
    - The Office of the Historian Milestones page (a retired series) says the Senate 'ratified the agreement on April 28, 1818'; Hunter Miller gives Senate approval on 16 April and the proclamation on 28 April 1818. Hunter Miller is followed.
    - The 1892 report (Senate Executive Document No. 9, 52nd Congress, 2nd session) and House Document No. 471, 56th Congress, 1st session were not retrieved; they would be the fullest official source for nineteenth-century compliance questions.
    - The Canadian ID E101662 text and details pages are dated 'modified 2019-03-01'.
    - Checked by the main session, 11 Oct 2026: both notes' terms and paragraph numbering against Canada E101662 and the 1818 proclamation; the 23 November 1864 notice and 9 February 1865 joint resolution against 13 Stat. 568 (govinfo); current status against US Treaties in Force 2025, Canada section. The 1864 notice was recoded from Denunciation to Withdrawal notice, since it was withdrawn before taking effect.
    - 11 Oct 2026: the 'Interprets' relation now exists; the 1939 exchange is record AC-127 and interprets this record. The 1940, 1942 and 1946 exchanges are to follow as separate records.
    - 11 Oct 2026: the 1946 date was corrected after the 1939 research. Canada's text prints the US note as 5 December 1946; UNTS vol. 149 prints 6 December 1946. The event is shown as '5 or 6 December 1946'.

## Correction to earlier entry: Rush-Bagot: corrections to the earlier entry

    - The title 'Rush-Bagot Treaty' is a common general description; the form code is exchange of notes, and the title now follows the official name. ('Treaty' is not wrong as a description of a binding international agreement.)
    - Date Adopted and Opened for Signature hold the range 28 to 29 April 1817. The range correctly reflects the two signature dates, but the coding uses 29 April 1817, when the exchange was completed and the agreement entered into force; a bilateral exchange of notes is not opened for signature.
    - Date Entered into Force is empty; it should be 29 April 1817 (US Treaties in Force 2025; Canada Treaty Information).
    - All substantive properties (parties, provisions, withdrawal clause, URL, word count) are empty.
    - The page body holds two duplicate copies of the old empty section template.
    - The page-body date note cites the Avalon Project text, whose header misdates the British note as 16 April 1817; the correct date is 28 April 1817.
